## Where this comes from

I ran monthly payroll, company and individual taxation, bank reconciliation,
expense control and attendance reporting as Finance Associate at NaqCoDE
Technologies from October 2024 to August 2025 — exactly the period Pakistan
calls Tax Year 2025 — and supported the senior auditor in preparing the
financial statements.

That work stayed on the company's systems and **none of it appears here**. This
project rebuilds the same problems from scratch on invented data, so the method
can be shown without disclosing anything about a former employer or its staff.
Every employee, salary, absence and claim below is simulated; the tax rules are
real.

## The payroll bridge

![Payroll bridge](/images/projects/pk-payroll-bridge.png)

Contractual salary is not what reaches the bank. Across the simulated year:
**PKR 57.6m contracted**, 0.6m lost to unpaid absence, 0.9m added in overtime,
**6.4m withheld in income tax** and 0.1m in EOBI, leaving **51.6m paid**.

All twelve monthly registers reconcile — earned gross less deductions equals net
pay, per employee and in total — and the report refuses to continue if a month
does not tie. A payroll you cannot reconcile is a payroll you should not pay.

## The tax curve, and a table that checks itself

![Marginal and effective rate](/images/projects/pk-tax-rate-curve.png)

Pakistan's salaried slabs are purely marginal, so unlike Ireland's USC there is
no income at which earning one more rupee leaves you worse off — a test asserts
that at every slab boundary.

The more interesting test is on the slab table itself. The fixed amounts
(30,000 / 180,000 / 430,000 / 700,000) are not hard-coded a second time as
expected values: the suite recomputes each one from the marginal rates below it.
A typo in the table fails the tests instead of quietly mispaying everyone.

## Why a raise doubles the tax line

One employee gets a 35% raise in January. Their **monthly tax deduction rises
101%** — it doubles — because section 149 withholding spreads the whole year's
liability across twelve months, and the raise moves them from the 15% slab into
the 25% one.

This is the question payroll gets asked every single time somebody is promoted.
It is also why the engine withholds on annual contractual salary rather than on
the month in hand.

## What attendance actually costs

![Unpaid absence by department](/images/projects/pk-attendance-cost.png)

| Department | Pay lost to unpaid leave | % of that team's payroll |
|---|---:|---:|
| Operations | 266,725 | 2.79% |
| Engineering | 131,386 | 0.66% |
| Sales | 89,120 | 0.65% |
| Finance | 75,930 | 0.53% |

Operations loses roughly four times the share the other teams do. That is a
number a manager can act on; a headline absence *rate* would not have shown the
cost.

Paid leave does not reduce pay, and unpaid leave is pro-rated on **working**
days rather than calendar days. Getting that distinction wrong is one of the
most common payroll errors, so it has its own test.

## Expense claims

266 claims across the year, 243 approved and 23 rejected. Every rejection
carries a reason that can be shown to the claimant — over the category limit,
no receipt above PKR 5,000, outside the 60-day window, or dated before the
expense was incurred. Anything above PKR 50,000 escalates to the finance head
rather than a line manager.

Each check is tested by feeding it the specific error it exists to catch, which
is the only way to know a validation still validates.

## Bank reconciliation

A reconciliation is not "do the two balances match" — they almost never do, and
that is not an error. The question is whether every difference can be **named**.
Whatever is left unnamed is the thing worth looking at.

The matcher runs two passes: exact reference first, then amount within a ten-day
clearing window, because a cheque written on the 28th may not clear until the
3rd. Reference matching goes first so two payments of the same value cannot be
swapped by a coincidental amount match — that has its own test.

What remains gets classified rather than listed: payments in the books that have
not reached the bank, lodgements the bank has not yet credited, and items on the
statement that never reached the books at all. That last group is the one that
needs a journal, and the check refuses to pass while any remain.

## The voucher control

This is the internal control I designed and ran, written down as code:

```
prepare  ->  approve  ->  disburse
(finance)    (director)   (CEO)
```

No one person can move money alone. The person who raises the voucher cannot
approve it; the person who approves it cannot release the cash.

Most of the module is refusals, because a control that cannot say no is not a
control. It blocks self-approval, the preparer or approver releasing payment,
disbursement before approval, approval above the director's PKR 100,000
authority without the CEO, a voucher with no narrative, and back-dating in
either direction.

The one I like most: **the approved amount is frozen at approval and re-checked
at payment**. Edit the figure after the director signs and it fails at the CEO
gate rather than quietly paying the larger amount.

There is also duplicate detection — same payee, same amount, within thirty days
— and a control summary reporting the segregation rate across disbursed
vouchers. That last number is what an auditor actually asks for: not that the
control exists, but that it operated.

## On the rules

Pinned to **Tax Year 2025** (Finance Act 2024) because that is the year I
worked. The Finance Act 2025 changed the slabs again for TY2026 — the 5% band
fell to 1% and the 15% band to 11% — so the slab table is versioned data rather
than constants scattered through the code, and another year can be added
without touching the calculation.

Slabs cross-checked against PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries, Befiler and EY's
Finance Bill 2024 alert. It is a model of the rules, not tax advice.
