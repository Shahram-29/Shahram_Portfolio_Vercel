## The recommendation

**Ryanair**, and the five-year record is the reason. Over April 2021 to April
2026, Ryanair returned **+42%** while easyJet lost roughly **60%** of its value.

![Cumulative returns](/images/projects/air-cumulative-returns.png)

The argument is not that Ryanair avoided trouble — it is that Ryanair kept
recovering from it. The Ukraine invasion, the Boeing delivery crisis, the Iran
escalation: each knocked the share price down and each time it came back higher
than before. That pattern traces to structural things rather than luck — owned
rather than leased aircraft, better fuel hedging, and a cleaner balance sheet.

By the end of the period Ryanair carried 200 million passengers in FY2025, had
returned over €9bn to shareholders since 2008, and traded around 18% below its
all-time high.

easyJet is genuinely better than it was in 2022 — debt cleared, the holidays
business growing, a record 2025 result. But the stock sits 60% below where it
was five years earlier and was approaching a **second death cross** on the
moving averages.

## The part I would actually talk about in an interview

The team ran a controlled test of whether AI can be trusted to compute financial
ratios, across Ryanair and easyJet for 2023 and 2024. Two methods, and the
difference between them is the whole lesson.

**Method 1 — no source documents.** The model was asked for the ratios directly,
leaving it to rely on training data and whatever it could scrape. Of **32 values,
8 matched** the hand calculations. It invented figures freely. easyJet's 2023
gearing came back as **2.2513 against a hand-calculated 0.4047** — out by more
than 1.8. ROE was off by 0.74. It got net margin right, and little else, because
net margin is the number printed everywhere.

**Method 2 — given the actual annual report PDFs.** Of **16 values, 9 matched**,
and net margin was correct 4 times out of 4.

The interesting part is what the remaining seven disagreements turned out to be.
**They were not hallucinations.** They were accounting judgment:

- **Gearing** — airlines lease aircraft at scale, and under IFRS 16 those leases
  are capitalised. The model included operating leases in the debt figure; the
  hand calculation did not. Both are defensible; they are not the same ratio.
- **Interest cover** — gross versus net interest expense, which are different
  numbers in a company that hedges.
- **Hedging** — jet fuel and currency hedges leave derivative accounting all
  through the statements, and a model will default to the simple treatment
  unless told otherwise.

The conclusion the team drew: grounding the model in the source documents
eliminates fabrication, but it does not eliminate disagreement — because gearing
has no single agreed formula, and two analysts will not match either. What is
needed is not just the PDFs but the **stated formula** for each ratio.

That is a more useful finding than "AI is unreliable", and it is the reason I
would raise this one rather than the recommendation.

## Method

- Ratio analysis across four families — liquidity and solvency, profitability,
  efficiency, and investment — computed by hand from the published annual
  reports for 2023 and 2024
- A five-year price study, April 2021 to April 2026, split into seven phases per
  airline and tied to news events
- 50/200-day moving averages and crossover signals
- Monthly return heatmaps, rolling volatility, drawdown and cumulative return
  comparison
- Bond and funding structure for both carriers, with issue sizes, coupons and
  credit ratings

5,602 words, fully referenced to the companies' annual reports, FAA and
Financial Times sources.
