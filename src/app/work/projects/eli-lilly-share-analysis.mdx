> MSc Business Analytics, Dublin Business School. January to February 2026.

## The question

How does a company that is executing well end up with a share price that
disagrees?

This is a five-year equity write-up on Eli Lilly, tracing its move from a
conventional large-cap pharmaceutical stock to a GLP-1 growth story, and then
the point at which record results stopped producing the reaction you would
expect.

## What the write-up covers

- **The tirzepatide pipeline**, and the approvals that re-rated the company —
  **Mounjaro** and **Zepbound**
- **Supply-constrained earnings misses** — the unusual position of missing
  expectations because you cannot manufacture fast enough, which is a very
  different signal from missing because demand is weak
- **Political pricing risk**, which sits over the whole category
- **The gap in early 2026** between record reported results and how the share
  price actually moved

## Why this one is different from the rest of my work

Everything else on this track is a model. This is a narrative argument built
from company filings, approval timelines and price history — no regression
anywhere in it.

That was deliberate. A forecast that cannot be explained in a paragraph to
somebody who does not code is not much use in an investment setting, and the
discipline of writing the case in prose first is what stops a model becoming a
substitute for understanding the business. The supply-constrained miss is the
clearest example: no purely statistical treatment of the earnings series would
distinguish it from an ordinary miss, but the distinction is the entire story.
